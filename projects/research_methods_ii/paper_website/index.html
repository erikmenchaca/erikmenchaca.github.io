Exploring the relationships among social media addiction, academic procrastination, anxiety, and GPA in college students.

The question

How do addiction-like social media use and academic procrastination relate to anxiety and grades—and do those relationships differ by sex?

No reliable differences were found.

Sex and sex-interaction effects were not statistically significant in the GPA or anxiety models.

These are associations, not evidence that one factor causes another.

02 · Method

One sample. Four perspectives.

01

Participants

84 RIT undergraduates ages 18–25 who regularly used social media and provided usable survey responses.

02

Design

A correlational survey measured associations and tested main effects and sex interactions with regression models.

03

Measures

BSMAS, APS-S, GAD-7, cumulative GPA, and brief academic performance questions.

04

Analysis

Pearson correlations, Welch’s independent-samples t-test, and multiple linear regression.

03 · Limitations

How to read these findings.

This study shows relationships between self-reported measures. It cannot establish that social media use or procrastination caused changes in anxiety or GPA.

Other factors may help explain these patterns. The sample and survey design also limit how broadly the results can be applied.

  • 84 students from one university; most were White
  • Self-reported social media use, anxiety, and GPA
  • Not enough statistical power to detect some small sex differences
  • No objective GPA or phone-use records
  • Sleep, workload, and work hours were not controlled

Next step: Study larger, more diverse groups over time and compare survey responses with phone-use and academic records.
